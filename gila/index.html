01 Why Gila Exists

A surname on the badge

Coach Ralph could have called it anything. He put his own family name on it instead — Gila — and that was the whole point. If his name is on the badge, the standard is his to answer for. There is nowhere to hide behind a nickname.

He had watched it happen for years at Rangers. A boy loses a match on Saturday, and everyone talks about Saturday. But the game was not lost on Saturday. It was lost months earlier, in the touches nobody practised, in the weak foot that got avoided, in the one-against-one he lost every single week and never once worked on.

Talent turns up on its own. Everything else has to be built, badly and repeatedly, until it holds.

The name turned out to be the plan as well. Grind, Improve, Learn, Achieve — four words that happen to spell the one already on the badge, in the exact order they have to be done.

So Gila is not really a team. It is a workshop with a badge on the door.

Nobody gets good on match day

Match day is the exam. It tells you what you already know, and it is far too late to learn anything new. The work happens on the quiet evenings when there is no crowd, no score, and nothing at stake but the next repetition.

That is the hour Gila is built around.

02 The Method

GILA spells the method

Four words, in order, and the badge is the first letter of each. They are a sequence, not a list — skip one and the rest stop working.

GGRIND

Turn up when it is boring. When it is hot, when the ball is worn, when nobody is watching. That is not the price of getting good — it is getting good.

IIMPROVE

One thing at a time, and measured. Not "get better at football" — fifty left-foot touches before the ball drops, and then fifty-one.

LLEARN

Understand why the drill exists, not just how to survive it. A player who knows the reason can coach themselves for the rest of their life.

AACHIEVE

Then, and only then, the match. Saturday stops being a test of nerve and becomes a report on the work already done.

Everyone wants the highlight. Almost nobody wants the hundredth repetition that made it possible.
— Coach Ralph, founder
03 The Work

What actually gets drilled

No secret method. Just the unglamorous things most players skip, done properly and often.

  • First touch under pressure

    Anyone can control a ball in space. The touch that matters is the one taken with somebody arriving.

  • The weak foot, until it isn't

    Half the pitch opens up the day a player stops shaping their whole body to avoid one side.

  • One against one

    Attacking it and defending it. The single most decided moment in youth football, and the least practised.

  • Finishing from awkward angles

    Because the ball almost never arrives the way it does in the drill everyone likes doing.

Why futsal

A smaller ball, a harder surface, four outfield players and nowhere to hide. Futsal forces a touch every few seconds and a decision with every touch — the same hour of training, several times the repetitions.

Players who come through futsal arrive on the full pitch already used to tight space. Everything suddenly feels slower, and slower is where good decisions live.

Gila runs both. They are not alternatives; they feed each other.

04 Who It's For

Come if you are willing to be bad at something first

Gila is open to any player already turning out for Rangers, FC New Mabu, a school team, or nobody at all. Beginners are welcome. So are the players who are already the best in their side and have quietly noticed that being the best in your side is not the same as being good.

The only requirement is a tolerance for looking clumsy in front of other people for a few weeks, which is the toll every skill charges on the way in.

05 The Squads

Two squads, one badge

The age groups already wearing it. Photographed at the end of the day on the same patch of ground they train on, which is exactly where a team photograph belongs.

U10

Old enough to be told the truth

Ten is the age at which a player can be told that their weak foot is the problem and treat it as a job rather than an insult. So this is where the counting starts — touches, repetitions, the number that has to be beaten next week. Most of them will look back on this as the year they first got better at something on purpose.

U8

Too young to know it's hard

Nobody in this group has been informed that football is difficult, and there is no hurry to tell them. The grind can wait a few years. For now the work is simpler, and matters more than any drill: touch the ball an enormous number of times, and love it enough to come back on Saturday.

Sky is the limit

It is written across the sky above them in the club's own hand. On most posters that line is decoration. Here it is simply accurate — almost nothing has happened to these players yet. Everything they will ever be good at is still in front of them, and all of it begins on this dirt, on an ordinary evening, with nobody watching.
